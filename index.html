COR3 Transparency Portal

A rebranded public window into Puerto Rico’s recovery—combining complex financial data, project progress, educational content, procurement visibility, and accountability in one responsive experience.

Visit the Transparency Portal ↗
MY PROCESS

Designing for public understanding

I treated the Transparency Portal as an information-clarity problem, not only a dashboard problem. The process connected audience needs, recovery terminology, content hierarchy, visual trust, and data interaction into one public experience.

1 · Understand

I began with the questions different audiences bring: Where is the money going? What is happening near me? Which agency or program is responsible? What stage is a project in?

2 · Structure

I organized disasters, programs, finances, contracts, documents, and project details around those public tasks—using plain-language pathways before exposing deeper data.

3 · Make data legible

I translated funding totals, disbursement trends, project stages, maps, and agency relationships into consistent visual patterns with context, labels, and drill-down paths.

4 · Prototype and validate

I used wireframes and clickable flows to test navigation, filtering, terminology, and comprehension, then refined the interface in collaboration with delivery teams.

5 · Design for change

Because recovery information evolves, the solution included reusable content patterns and a tailored CMS. The design could expand to new disasters, programs, documents, and reporting needs without rebuilding the experience from scratch.

The challenge

After Hurricanes Irma and María, Puerto Rico faced a long, expensive recovery across damaged power, water, communications, and basic services. Public trust depended on making government-led recovery activity transparent, understandable, and accessible.

The solution

The redesigned portal brought together financial data and descriptive content so the public could understand how funds were obligated and disbursed, how projects moved through recovery, and where to find procurement and accountability information.

Experience pillars

  • Educational explanations of recovery programs.
  • Interactive infographics for complex processes.
  • Visibility into RFPs, contracts, and procurement.
  • Direct access to report fraud, waste, or abuse.

Responsive public access

The experience was designed for desktop, tablet, and mobile and supported major browsers. Flexible tables, custom visualizations, an island-wide financial map, and project-stage diagrams made recovery information easier to explore.

A content system built for continuous recovery

The tailored content management system allowed teams to create, edit, and publish pages, images, and video without specialized technical knowledge. It included a searchable document library, reusable content cards, FAQs, interactive flow diagrams, newsroom publishing, and a public glossary.

Documented scale

According to the supplied case study, since the 2018 launch the number of non-FEMA Federal Recovery Fund metrics increased by 175%, while FEMA Recovery Fund metrics increased by 325%.

Broader recovery scope

The portal later expanded beyond Hurricanes Irma and María to include content and data related to earthquakes, the coronavirus pandemic, and Hurricane Fiona—making the content architecture’s adaptability part of the product value.

Outcome narrative

The case study attributes four broader benefits to the platform: increased transparency into public spending, better-informed public decision-making, enhanced accountability through visibility, and more streamlined recovery and procurement processes. These are presented as documented case-study outcomes rather than independently audited causal findings.

Designing for Complexity: Making Enterprise Experiences Feel Simple

Simplicity is not the absence of complexity. It is the result of structuring complexity around what a person needs, when they need it.

Complexity→Structure→Clarity

By Aneesha Watve

“Make it simple” is one of the most common requests designers receive. It is also one of the most misunderstood.

Simple does not mean removing information until the screen looks clean. In complex enterprise products, simplicity is something much harder: making a complicated system understandable without pretending that the complexity does not exist.

This has been one of the defining challenges of my enterprise UX work.

Complexity has many sources

An enterprise workflow may need to accommodate multiple user roles, different permissions, regulatory requirements, legacy processes, exceptions, large datasets, dependencies between teams, technical constraints, and years of accumulated business rules.

None of that disappears simply because we want a cleaner interface.

The challenge is therefore not, “How can I make this screen simple?” It is, “How can I help this user deal with only the complexity that matters to them, at the moment it matters?” That is a very different design problem.

Start with the user's mental model

Organizations tend to describe products according to how the organization is structured. Users rarely think that way.

A system might internally consist of modules, databases, departments, programs, and approval stages. The user is thinking: “I need to submit this.” “I need to know what happened.” “I need to fix this problem.” “I need to make a decision.”

One of the designer's most important jobs is translating organizational complexity into a structure aligned with the user's goal. That translation affects navigation, information architecture, terminology, workflow, interaction patterns, and content.

Progressive complexity

Not every user needs every piece of information at once. This is where progressive disclosure becomes powerful.

Show what is necessary for the current decision. Make secondary information available when needed. Reveal advanced functionality in context. Provide meaningful defaults. Use hierarchy to distinguish the critical from the merely available.

The system can remain sophisticated underneath while the user's interaction with it remains understandable. That is not “dumbing down” the product. It is good information architecture.

Visual design comes later than structural clarity

My foundation is in visual communication, and I continue to care deeply about typography, composition, hierarchy, and aesthetics. But complex products have repeatedly taught me that visual polish cannot rescue structural confusion.

Before asking whether a screen looks good, I want to know: Does the user understand where they are? Do they know what they can do? Do they know what requires attention? Do they understand what happens next? Can they recover from a mistake? Does the terminology match their mental model?

Only then does visual design amplify the experience.

Simple is an outcome

Some of the best interface work is almost invisible. Users do not notice the information architecture. They do not see the interaction model. They do not know that ten edge cases were resolved behind a single workflow. They simply accomplish what they came to do.

That is why one of my favorite ways to describe the work I do is: Complex → Radically Simple.

Not because the underlying problem became simple. Because careful research, architecture, interaction design, systems thinking, accessibility, and iteration absorbed enough of the complexity that the user no longer had to.

And perhaps that is one of the most valuable things good UX can do.

UX Research Starts Before You Talk to a User

A conversation is not automatically research. Good research begins with the architecture of the study and the decision it must inform.

Question→Evidence→Decision

By Aneesha Watve

User research sounds straightforward. Talk to users. Observe them. Ask questions. Identify pain points. Translate those insights into design.

In practice, meaningful qualitative research is much harder.

One of the most important ideas reinforced through my UX research studies is that good research does not begin with the interview. It begins with the architecture of the research itself.

A conversation is not automatically research

It is easy to conduct an interesting user interview. It is harder to conduct one that reliably helps a team make a decision.

Before speaking with a participant, I want to understand: What are we trying to learn? What decision will this research inform? What do we already know? What assumptions are we testing? Who can provide the evidence we need? Which research method fits the question?

Those decisions shape the quality of everything that follows. Without them, teams can collect pages of observations and still struggle to answer the original product question.

Ask about behavior, not just preference

There is an important difference between asking people what they like and understanding what they actually do.

Consider: “Would you use a feature that automatically organizes these items?” versus “Tell me about the last time you needed to organize these items. What did you do?”

The first invites speculation. The second begins with real behavior. People are usually much better witnesses of their past experiences than predictors of their future behavior.

The insight is not the quote

Another common mistake is treating memorable user statements as insights. A quote is evidence. An insight requires interpretation.

Imagine several users saying, “I export this information into Excel.” The superficial conclusion might be: “Users want Excel export.” Perhaps. But deeper investigation might reveal that users export because the product does not let them compare records, perform calculations, share information, or create a required downstream view.

The real opportunity may have little to do with Excel. Qualitative analysis moves from individual observations to patterns, from patterns to meaning, and from meaning to implications for the product.

Research should create decisions, not reports

A polished presentation with personas, quotes, photographs, and findings can still have limited impact if nobody knows what to do next.

I prefer research that answers: What did we learn? How confident are we? Which assumption changed? What should the team reconsider? Which opportunity deserves further exploration? What do we still not know?

The output might be a report. But the outcome should be a better decision.

Research is an architecture

I increasingly think of qualitative research as an architecture rather than an activity. The interview is one component. So are participant selection, research questions, methodology, facilitation, analysis, synthesis, bias awareness, communication, and decision-making.

When those elements reinforce one another, research becomes much more than “talking to users.” It becomes a disciplined way of reducing uncertainty.

And that is when research begins to influence product strategy rather than merely document user opinion.

UX Leadership Is Less About Authority and More About Alignment

Some of the most important UX leadership moments happen when nobody on the project reports to you.

Perspectives→Alignment→Direction

By Aneesha Watve

For a long time, I associated design leadership with managing designers. Today, I see it differently.

A product manager has one priority. Engineering has another. Business stakeholders focus on deadlines or outcomes. Compliance teams see risks. Users simply want to accomplish what they came to do. Design sits somewhere in the middle.

One of my biggest takeaways from studying UX management is that UX leadership is often an alignment problem before it is a design problem.

Great design rarely fails because someone cannot draw the interface

Enterprise design teams generally know how to create wireframes, prototypes, components, and interfaces. The harder problems are usually elsewhere.

What problem are we actually solving? Which user need should take priority? Which requirement is truly mandatory? Where can we simplify? What tradeoff are we willing to make? Who has the authority to decide?

Unless those questions are resolved, producing more screens does not necessarily move the product forward. Sometimes it simply makes disagreement more visual.

The designer as translator

Throughout my career, I have increasingly found myself playing the role of translator—not language translation, but perspective translation.

A business stakeholder may describe a requirement through a business objective. An engineer may describe the same requirement through system behavior. A compliance team may frame it around risk. A user may describe the resulting problem as, “I don't know what I'm supposed to do next.”

All four perspectives can be valid. The designer's job is to create a shared understanding of the experience connecting them.

Journey maps, workflows, prototypes, service blueprints, research findings, and even a well-framed design question can become alignment tools. The artifact is valuable, but the conversation it enables is often even more valuable.

Influence is a design skill

Presenting a design is not simply explaining why a button is placed somewhere. It means explaining the reasoning behind a product decision: What problem did we observe? What evidence do we have? What alternatives were considered? What tradeoffs are involved? What would success look like?

Once the conversation moves to that level, design critique becomes less subjective. Instead of “I like option A,” the discussion becomes, “Option A better supports the workflow we agreed is most important.” That shift matters.

Leadership can happen without the title

I have seen this outside professional product teams as well. While coordinating a volunteer educational program, I worked with board members, teachers, parents, students, and volunteers who often had very different priorities.

There was no organizational hierarchy that could solve every disagreement. Progress depended on communication, structure, shared goals, feedback mechanisms, and trust.

UX organizations operate in surprisingly similar ways. You cannot mandate genuine alignment. You have to create the conditions for it.

My definition of UX leadership today

UX leadership is not simply producing the strongest design. It is helping a group of people make better experience decisions together.

Sometimes that requires research. Sometimes a prototype. Sometimes a framework. Sometimes a difficult conversation. And sometimes the most important contribution a designer can make is not another screen at all.

It is helping everyone finally see the same problem.

Why I Went Back to the Classroom After 18+ Years in Design

I did not go back to learn how to design. I went back to understand design—and my own experience—more deeply.

Experience→Frameworks→Intentional practice

By Aneesha Watve

After more than 18 years in design, I decided to go back to the classroom. Not because I was starting over. Because I wanted to understand my own experience better.

My career has moved through visual communication, advertising, digital learning, branding, UX, product design, consulting, and complex enterprise platforms. With every transition, my role became less about simply creating something visually effective and more about understanding users, solving complex problems, aligning stakeholders, and helping teams make better product decisions.

Experience had given me strong instincts—but I wanted stronger frameworks behind those instincts. That led me to complete my UX Certification with Nielsen Norman Group, with advanced learning focused on UX management, product strategy, qualitative research, and UX metrics.

Why study UX after already practicing it for years?

After working in design for so long, I had already encountered ambiguous requirements, competing priorities, complex workflows, frustrated users, design-system challenges, accessibility considerations, difficult trade-offs, and the tension between user needs, business goals, and technical constraints.

But experiencing something and having a structured way to reason about it are not always the same thing. I wanted to step away from deadlines and deliverables and examine fundamental questions more deeply.

How should research be structured so it influences product decisions? How do we move UX conversations beyond individual screens? How should designers collaborate with product and engineering? How do we measure whether an experience improved? And as designers become more senior, how does the role evolve from designing the experience to shaping the conditions in which good experiences get created?

Experience gave me patterns. NN/g gave me another lens.

One benefit of working in design for many years is pattern recognition. You sense when a workflow is unnecessarily complicated. You recognize when stakeholders describe symptoms instead of the underlying problem. You can anticipate where users may struggle before formal testing.

That intuition is valuable. But it can become dangerous if we stop questioning it.

The NN/g certification gave me an opportunity to revisit familiar problems through structured frameworks. The learning helped me articulate why something had worked—or why it had not.

Once something becomes explicit rather than intuitive, it becomes easier to explain, repeat, teach, challenge, and improve.

My questions have changed over the years

Early in my career, many questions were about execution: How should this information be presented? What should the visual hierarchy be? How can we communicate the idea more clearly? Those questions still matter deeply.

Over time, additional questions became equally important: Are we solving the right problem? What evidence tells us it matters? Whose perspective might be missing? What assumptions are we making? What trade-offs are we accepting? How does the experience connect with the wider product ecosystem? How will we know whether the design improved anything?

That shift—from focusing primarily on the artifact to thinking about the system surrounding it—has been one of the biggest evolutions in my career.

Enterprise UX made this especially clear

My recent work has involved complex enterprise experiences where design decisions are rarely isolated. A seemingly simple screen may sit at the intersection of customer needs, business processes, regulatory requirements, accessibility standards, engineering constraints, multiple user roles, and legacy systems.

In those environments, UX cannot be reduced to making an interface easier to use. It requires systems thinking, research, experience architecture, product thinking, design systems, measurement, communication, and alignment between people who see the same problem from very different perspectives.

My NN/g learning helped me connect many of these pieces more deliberately.

Going back to the classroom was also humbling

It reminded me how important it is to remain a student. When you have worked in a field for a long time, it is easy to become comfortable with your methods. Formal learning creates an opportunity to question them.

Some concepts reinforced things I already believed. Others gave me new vocabulary for ideas developed through experience. Some challenged assumptions I had carried for years. I found that refreshing.

Design keeps changing. Products change. Technology changes. User expectations and organizations change. AI is changing both the products we design and the way designers work.

Remaining curious is not separate from being an experienced designer. I increasingly believe it is part of the responsibility.

I didn't go back to learn how to design

I went back to understand design more deeply. My NN/g certification did not replace what 18+ years of practice had taught me. It helped me organize, question, and strengthen it.

Earlier in my career, I often asked, “How can I design this better?” Today I ask a broader set of questions: Are we solving the right problem? What evidence supports our decision? How does this experience fit into the larger system? How will we know whether it worked? How can I help the entire team make better experience decisions?

Going back to the classroom was not about adding another credential. It was about becoming more intentional about the designer I want to be in the next chapter of my career.
